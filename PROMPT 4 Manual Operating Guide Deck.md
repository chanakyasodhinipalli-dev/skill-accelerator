# PROMPT 4 — Manual Approach · Operating Guide (triage leads, shared pool, squad leads)

Copy everything below the line into a fresh session.

---

Build me a PowerPoint deck (.pptx) for a working session with triage leads, a shared enablement pool and squad leads. Title slide plus **10 content slides**. Dense, infographic-led, data-carrying — this is the team that will execute it, so they need the mechanics, not the pitch.

The deck explains how to run a tiered partner-enablement model **entirely by hand** for one quarter, with nothing built, and what must be measured while it runs. **Do not describe, propose, cost or allude to any tooling, automation or AI programme anywhere in this deck** — not as a future phase, not as a comparison column, not as a backlog. The subject is how people run this model manually, and nothing else.

## Context you are working from

I lead work on an enterprise ECM platform team. ECM provides repository services to partner applications for storage, archival and records management, with metadata compliance and document classification applied on arrival. Three scrum teams, thirty-plus engineers, every intake delivered through a dedicated capacity model from inception to release.

**The structural problem:** capacity required = concurrent intakes × a fixed engineering depth applied to every intake. Both terms outside our control, so headcount is the only lever. An allocation problem, not a throughput problem.

**Two causes.** (1) *The data deficit* — the requirement for a data point is discovered a step at a time, after the intake has landed; squads estimate 30–40% of elapsed time; tier-independent, so no re-tiering fixes it; the most draining work they do. (2) *Uniform allocation* — of 38 scenarios, 8 need no design, 14 need a judgement, 14 need a pod, 2 are campaigns; all 38 get the same team; without an objective rule, depth is allocated by influence.

**The model:** Lean (delivery on a proven pattern, no allocated capacity), Lean+ (shared pool, two mandatory design checkpoints, named guide), Dedicated (allocated pod, inception to release), Migration Factory (remediation as campaigns). Tier assigned by ECM against a published rubric, never chosen by the partner.

**Why by hand.** Every mechanism the model depends on has a human equivalent we can stand up in a fortnight, and the knowledge already sits with the leads in this room. What is missing is a written rule, calibration, and the authority to apply it. None of the numbers that would justify doing it any other way exist yet — not the distribution against live demand, not the effort baseline, not the elapsed-time baseline, not the size of the clerical load. Running it by hand produces all of them as a by-product.

## Slide-by-slide specification

**1 — Why we are doing this by hand.** Navy band with the capacity equation, the fixed-depth term in accent colour. Two cause cards (the data deficit; uniform allocation), each with five supporting points. Then a card headed "Why by hand, and why now" with four reasons: the knowledge is already here, not somewhere else; none of the numbers that describe our own problem currently exist; a written rule and delegated authority are decisions, not work; and if the model turns out to be wrong we unwind a practice rather than an asset. Closing note: this quarter is practice plus measurement — the discipline is what is new, not the capability.

**2 — Every mechanism the model needs, done by people.** A 14-row table: *capability the model needs / how we do it by hand / what that costs us*. Rows: guided intake → structured triage conversation worked from a written guide (the partner cannot work ahead unaided); the data catalogue and why-register → the same content as a written guide the lead works from (nothing material lost); completeness validation → lead scores against published bands (no hard block, so an incomplete intake can still proceed if nobody holds the line); gap register → a gaps table in the tracker (no ageing, no automatic chasing — someone must chase); handoff contract → a one-page Triage Outcome Record issued by the lead; tier determination → lead scores seven determinants on a sheet (consistency depends on calibration); pattern matching → the lead's recall plus a written pattern log (depends on who is in the room); scope derivation → a scope checklist per pattern; gate enforcement → a gate checklist walked by lead and pool (gates can be waived — the central risk); partner visibility → one hand-maintained tracker (partners ask us instead of looking); artefact preparation → templates filled in by hand (the clerical load remains almost entirely); support triage → the pool's channel plus published office hours (works well, little lost); pattern capture → a pattern log, one page per delivery; drift detection → fortnightly review against a written trigger list (up to two weeks' delay before drift is caught). Accent note: keep a running record of which of these losses actually hurt in practice, with examples and dates — judgement at the end of the quarter must rest on that record, not on recollection.

**3 — The Triage Guide.** Eight section cards in two columns: the tier model; the seven determinants; thresholds and overrides; the question set; completeness bands; guardrails by tier; escalation triggers; templates. A navy band stating the effort — roughly one week of two experienced engineers plus a governance analyst. Then the 54-point question set as ten small category tiles with counts: A Business context 5, B Application & zone 6, C Document inventory 8, D Trust & classification 5, E Retention & regulatory 6, F Channel & integration 7, G Metadata & retrieval 5, H Security & users 4, I Environments & cutover 4, J Support & ownership 4. Closing note on what each catalogue entry carries: the question, who answers it, why it is required, what breaks downstream without it, diagnostic sub-questions for when the partner does not know, and which patterns it applies to.

**4 — The deeper triage conversation.** A four-step chevron chain: pre-read issued → 90-minute Discovery Triage with the partner → 30-minute internal Tiering Review → Outcome Record issued. Then four behaviour rows — explains the why at the point of asking (give the document-traits example in full: why we need to know whether documents arrive as born-digital PDFs or scans, what that determines downstream for classification and trust posture, and what happens if we find out in week six instead); treats "I don't know" as a normal state with a defined path (record it as a dated, owned gap, name who will find out, say what it blocks); surfaces contradictions immediately rather than noting them politely; flags long-lead items on the day they are identified. A right-hand card listing the eight elements of the Triage Outcome Record. An accent band at the foot: this is the change that carries most of the benefit — the full requirement established in one conversation, with everything not yet knowable converted from an unknown-unknown into a scheduled known. Nothing is automated; the clarification cycle is removed by asking earlier and better.

**5 — Tier assignment: the lead decides.** The seven determinants as a 0–3 scoring table (D1 pattern precedent, D2 content trust posture, D3 regulatory and records, D4 classification engine, D5 data model and metadata, D6 volume and performance, D7 breadth of impact). Thresholds as three coloured pills (0–5 Lean, 6–11 Lean+, 12–21 Dedicated). Hard overrides table: no precedent at all → Dedicated; existing content or metadata to be remediated → Dedicated; new regulated retention or WORM → Dedicated; multiple LOBs or partners in one intake → Dedicated; partner not yet through one prior intake → not Lean. Then four calibration cards — before we start (two half-days scoring the same three historical intakes independently, then reconciling); first two months (fortnightly review of every assignment as a group); every deviation recorded with a reason, because a determinant repeatedly overridden in the same direction is a rubric defect and not a discipline problem; appeal (one per intake, five working days, must name the determinant in dispute). Closing note: the lead also selects and records which guardrails apply to that intake, and every waiver is recorded with who authorised it.

**6 — The shared pool.** Three composition cards: permanent core of 2 (continuity and pattern memory), rotating members 2–4 (six-to-eight-week secondments — spreads the knowledge and means nobody is permanently removed from their squad), total 4–6 (sized against aggregate checkpoint load, not partner count). Then an operating-rhythm table: Lean+ checkpoints (two mandatory, booked in advance; no artefact means no review, rebook); office hours (fixed published slots, first come first served); escalation duty (one member per week, one-working-day response); named guide (a contact and a reviewer, not a delivery resource, carrying several intakes at once); fortnightly drift review; pattern log upkeep. An accent band: the pool must be protected and it will be tested — the first time a squad reclaims its person and it is allowed, the pool becomes notional and Lean+ reverts to unsupported self-service; protection must be an explicit commitment with a named escalation route agreed before we start. Closing note: a guide who starts doing the implementation has converted a Lean+ intake into an unfunded Dedicated one, and that is the single easiest way for this quarter to fail quietly.

**7 — What we can actually reach by hand.** Two stacked segmented bars: Today (38 Dedicated) and Running by hand (5 Lean, 17 Lean+, 14 Dedicated, 2 Factory), with a legend. Two cards: why Lean+ works by hand (a human process by design — booked checkpoints, a named contact, scheduled design assurance; everything it depends on can be done by people with a calendar) and why Lean stays narrow (it assumes the partner can proceed unaided — pick up a proven pattern, provision inside policy bounds, clear their own gates, see their own state; with every step mediated by a person, only the five simplest and most-repeated patterns can run that way without the lead effectively doing the work for them). A navy band: seventeen of the twenty-two lighter-tier scenarios therefore land in Lean+, which is where the bulk of the gain comes from this quarter. Accent note: this reallocates depth, it does not remove work; the clerical load remains almost entirely; per-intake effort stays roughly constant as demand grows — say all three out loud rather than letting the room discover them in week eight.

**8 — The tracker and the pattern log.** The tracker as an eight-row table of column groups: identity; tiering (determinant scores, total, overrides applied, assigned tier, deviation reason); pattern; completeness band; gaps (owner, due date, blocking gate); gates (P0–P5 status and clearance dates); measurement (clarification cycles counted, stage entry and exit dates, effort by team); escalation. An accent band: the measurement columns are not administrative overhead — they are the primary output of this quarter; one named owner, reviewed weekly, and if the tracker decays we end the quarter with a process change and no evidence that it worked. Then the pattern log as five tiles: what the partner needed; what was configured; what varied from the pattern; what proved difficult; how long each phase took. Closing note: one page per delivery, written the week it completes, because nobody reconstructs this accurately a month later.

**9 — Guardrails under a manual regime.** A strip of eight invariants that no tier exempts: classification determination, retention approval, security and access review, volume certification, promotion ladder, support acceptance, audit trail, new document type re-review. Then a table — *guardrail / who enforces it / how it is enforced by hand / the weak point* — covering classification determination (Imaging Governance, on the flow), retention approval (records management sign-off before go-live), security and access routing, volume certification before load testing, the promotion ladder, new document type (caught at the fortnightly drift review, so up to two weeks late), and audit trail (recorded by the lead in the tracker, not captured automatically). An accent band: the central discipline risk — a written rule applied by a person under schedule pressure is weaker than a gate that blocks; guardrails will be waived; the defence is visibility, not prohibition, so every waiver is recorded with who authorised it and reviewed fortnightly. Then four watch-item cards with a named mitigation each: deep triage gets shortened when the calendar is tight; the pool is reclaimed by squads; the tracker decays around week five; Lean is attempted too widely.

**10 — The twelve weeks.** A four-step chevron chain: weeks 1–2 Prepare / 3–6 Run in parallel / 7–10 Operate the tiers / 11–12 Evidence review, each with its activities. A note that the parallel run must not be shortened — it produces real data at zero delivery risk and gives the leads four weeks of scoring practice before assignments bind real work. Then the evidence review as a seven-row table (criterion / what good looks like / what it proves): distribution holds (50%+ to Lean or Lean+); lighter tiers hold (escalation back to Dedicated below 25%); clarification cycles fall against the baseline; partners accept assigned tiering (appeal below 20%, most unsuccessful); the baseline exists (effort and elapsed time, per tier, per phase); the bottleneck is identified — a quantified statement of where manual effort concentrates; no compliance regression. A navy band: we are measuring our own problem for the first time, so the fifth and sixth criteria matter as much as the first.

## Design system

- 13.3 × 7.5 inches (`LAYOUT_WIDE`). Margins 0.45" left and right.
- Palette — navy `15304F` (primary, Dedicated), teal `0B6E6E` (Lean), amber `9A6410` (Lean+), plum `5B4A72` (Factory), rust `A44A2A` (accent / alert), green `2E6B4F` (positive), ink `1B2432`, muted `55606E`, slate `7C8798`, line `D4DAE2`, panel `F4F7FA`. Tints: `E6F2F1` / `FAF1DF` / `E8EDF4` / `F1EEF6` / `FBF0EB` / `E9EEF4`.
- Fonts — Cambria for titles and big numbers, Calibri for everything else. Nothing else.
- Every tier keeps the same colour on every slide, without exception. Tier cells in tables get a tinted background and coloured bold text.
- Consistent slide header: a small numbered navy square, a 24pt Cambria title, a 10.5pt muted subtitle, a right-aligned section tag (Context / Doing it by hand / Preparation / The new triage / Tiering / The pool / What we reach / Measurement / Guardrails / The plan), and a hairline rule beneath.
- Title slide: full navy background, a teal vertical bar at the far left edge, kicker in letter-spaced caps, large Cambria title, subtitle, a short teal rule, four metadata columns, and a footer rule. Keep the title short enough for one line.
- Rounded rectangles (radius ~0.05), soft shadows, hairline borders. No gradients, no accent stripes under titles, no decorative colour bars.

## Build instructions

Use `pptxgenjs` in Node. Set `pres.layout` before adding slides. Hex colours with **no** `#` and no alpha. Build a fresh options object for every `addShape`/`addText` — never share one. Shadow offsets must be ≥ 0. Use `charSpacing`, not `letterSpacing`. Put `isTextBox: true` on every `addText` and `margin: 0` wherever text must align to a shape edge. `rectRadius` only works on `ROUNDED_RECTANGLE`.

Write a shared helper library first — slide header, stat tile, card, segmented bar, legend, chevron chain, table (with tier-cell colouring), note band, callout band, section label — and compose all ten slides from it.

**Speaker notes are mandatory on every slide** via `slide.addNotes()`. Write them for someone running a working session with the team that will execute this: the mechanics, what to emphasise, where to invite challenge, and the specific instruction to give the room. Several hundred words per slide.

**QA before handing it over:** convert to PDF, render every slide to an image and inspect each one. Check first for text overflowing or cut off at a shape boundary — especially the last wrapped line inside bordered callout boxes, which is by far the most common failure. Then check for tables or cards colliding with the band below them, section labels wrapping onto the content beneath, content off the slide edge, and badges taller than their table rows. Fix everything found and re-render to confirm.

## Tone

Practitioner register — this is an operating guide, not a pitch. Be direct about what will be hard: that two leads will score the same intake differently at first, that guardrails will be waived under pressure, that the pool will be reclaimed unless it is explicitly protected, that the tracker will feel like overhead by week five, and that a named guide drifting into implementation work is the quiet failure mode. Address each with a named mitigation rather than optimism. End by asking the room to keep a running record of where working by hand actually hurt — with examples and dates — because that record and the tracker are the only two things the week-12 review can be run on, and neither can be reconstructed from memory afterwards.
