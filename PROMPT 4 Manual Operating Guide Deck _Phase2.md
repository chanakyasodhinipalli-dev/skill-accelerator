# PROMPT 4 — Phase 1 Manual Approach · Operating Guide (triage leads, shared pool, squad leads)

Copy everything below the line into a fresh session.

---

Build me a PowerPoint deck (.pptx) for a working session with triage leads, a shared enablement pool and squad leads. Title slide plus **10 content slides**. Dense, infographic-led, data-carrying — this is the team that will execute it, so they need the mechanics, not the pitch.

The deck explains how to run a tiered partner-enablement model **by hand**, with no tooling built, for one quarter — and what must be measured while it runs.

## Context you are working from

I lead work on an enterprise ECM platform team. ECM provides repository services to partner applications for storage, archival and records management, with metadata compliance and document classification applied on arrival. Three scrum teams, thirty-plus engineers, every intake delivered through a dedicated capacity model from inception to release.

**The structural problem:** capacity required = concurrent intakes × a fixed engineering depth applied to every intake. Both terms outside our control, so headcount is the only lever. An allocation problem, not a throughput problem.

**Two causes.** (1) *The data deficit* — the requirement for a data point is discovered a step at a time, after the intake has landed; squads estimate 30–40% of elapsed time; tier-independent; the most draining work they do. (2) *Uniform allocation* — of 38 scenarios, 8 need no design, 14 need a judgement, 14 need a pod, 2 are campaigns; all 38 get the same team; without an objective rule, depth is allocated by influence.

**The model:** Lean (self-service on a proven pattern, no allocated capacity), Lean+ (shared pool, two mandatory design checkpoints, named guide), Dedicated (allocated pod, inception to release), Migration Factory (remediation as campaigns). Tier assigned by ECM against a published rubric, never chosen by the partner.

**The sequencing:** run it manually for a quarter, then build what the evidence says to build. Every number in the business case for a platform — the distribution, the effort baseline, the elapsed-time baseline, the size of the clerical load — does not currently exist, and running by hand produces all of them.

## Slide-by-slide specification

**1 — Why we are doing this by hand first.** Navy band with the capacity equation. Two cause cards (the data deficit; uniform allocation), each with five supporting points. Then a comparison table — "Build first" versus "Prove first (what we are doing)" — across sequence, what the business case rests on, the baseline, what happens if the model is wrong, and the Phase 2 backlog. Closing note on the sequencing argument.

**2 — Every platform mechanism has a human equivalent.** A 14-row substitution table: platform mechanism / manual equivalent / what is lost. Rows: intake copilot → structured triage conversation from a written guide (partner cannot self-serve); data catalogue and why-register → the same content as a written guide (nothing material lost); completeness validation → lead scores against published bands (no automatic block); gap register → a gaps table (no ageing or chasing); handoff contract → a one-page Triage Outcome Record; tiering assistant → lead scores seven determinants on a sheet; pattern matcher → lead's recall plus a pattern log; scope derivation → a scope checklist per pattern; gate engine → a gate checklist walked by lead and pool (gates can be waived — the central risk); partner cockpit → one hand-maintained tracker; artefact generation → templates filled in (the clerical load remains almost entirely); clinic triage → the pool's channel plus office hours (works well); pattern catalogue → a pattern log, one page per delivery; drift detector → fortnightly review against the trigger list (up to two weeks' delay). Accent note: read the right-hand column as the Phase 2 backlog in draft, and keep a running record of which losses actually hurt.

**3 — The Triage Guide.** Eight section cards in two columns: the tier model; the seven determinants; thresholds and overrides; the question set; completeness bands; guardrails by tier; escalation triggers; templates. A navy band stating the effort — roughly one week of two experienced engineers plus a governance analyst, and the same asset the platform would have needed. Then the 54-point question set as ten small category tiles with counts: A Business context 5, B Application & zone 6, C Document inventory 8, D Trust & classification 5, E Retention & regulatory 6, F Channel & integration 7, G Metadata & retrieval 5, H Security & users 4, I Environments & cutover 4, J Support & ownership 4. Closing note on what each catalogue entry carries, including diagnostic sub-questions and pattern applicability.

**4 — The deeper triage conversation.** A four-step chevron chain: pre-read issued → 90-minute Discovery Triage with the partner → 30-minute internal Tiering Review → Outcome Record issued. Then four behaviour rows — explains the why at the point of asking (with the document-traits example in full); treats "I don't know" as a normal state with a defined path; surfaces contradictions immediately; flags long-lead items on the day. A right-hand card listing the eight elements of the Triage Outcome Record. An accent band at the foot: the change that carries most of the Phase 1 benefit — the full requirement established in one conversation, with everything not yet knowable converted into a dated, owned item; nothing is automated, the cycle is removed by asking earlier and better.

**5 — Tier assignment: the lead decides.** The seven determinants as a 0–3 scoring table (D1 pattern precedent, D2 content trust posture, D3 regulatory and records, D4 classification engine, D5 data model and metadata, D6 volume and performance, D7 breadth of impact). Thresholds as three coloured pills (0–5 Lean, 6–11 Lean+, 12–21 Dedicated). Hard overrides table: no precedent at all → Dedicated; existing content or metadata remediated → Dedicated; new regulated retention or WORM → Dedicated; multiple LOBs or partners → Dedicated; partner not yet through one prior intake → not Lean. Then four calibration cards — before we start (two half-days scoring the same three historical intakes independently); first two months (fortnightly review of every assignment as a group); every deviation recorded with a reason, because a determinant repeatedly overridden in the same direction is a rubric defect not a discipline problem; appeal (one per intake, five days, must name the determinant). Closing note that the lead also selects and records the guardrails, and that every waiver is recorded with who authorised it.

**6 — The shared pool.** Three composition cards: permanent core of 2 (continuity and pattern memory), rotating members 2–4 (six-to-eight-week secondments, spreads knowledge, nobody permanently removed), total 4–6 (sized against aggregate checkpoint load, not partner count). Then an operating-rhythm table: Lean+ checkpoints (two mandatory, booked in advance, no artefact means no review, rebook); office hours (fixed published slots, first come first served); escalation duty (one member per week, one-working-day response); named guide (a contact, not a delivery resource, carrying several intakes); fortnightly drift review; pattern log. An accent band: the pool must be protected and it will be tested — the first time a squad reclaims its person and it is allowed, the pool becomes notional and Lean+ reverts to unsupported self-service; protection must be an explicit commitment with a named escalation route agreed before start. Closing note that a guide who starts doing implementation has converted a Lean+ intake into an unfunded Dedicated one.

**7 — What we can actually reach by hand.** Four stacked segmented bars: Today (38 Dedicated) / Phase 1 manual (5, 17, 14, 2) / Phase 2 at entry (8, 14, 14, 2) / Phase 2 steady (16, 13, 7, 2). Two cards: why Lean+ works by hand (a human process by design — everything it depends on can be done by people with a calendar) and why Lean largely does not (it assumes the partner can self-serve: instantiate a pattern, provision within policy bounds, clear automated gates, see their own state — without a platform they can do none of those). A navy band: eleven scenarios sit in that gap, and that is the clearest single statement of what Phase 2 buys. Accent note: Phase 1 reallocates depth, it does not remove work; the clerical load remains almost entirely; saying so now is what stops a successful Phase 1 becoming an argument against Phase 2.

**8 — The tracker and the pattern log.** The tracker as an eight-row table of column groups: identity; tiering (determinant scores, total, overrides, assigned tier, deviation reason); pattern; completeness; gaps (owner, due date, blocking gate); gates (P0–P5 status and clearance dates); measurement (clarification cycles counted, stage entry and exit dates, effort by team); escalation. An accent band: the measurement columns are not administrative overhead — they are the primary output of Phase 1, one named owner, reviewed weekly, and if the tracker decays we end the quarter with a process change and no evidence. Then the pattern log as five tiles: what the partner needed; what was configured; what varied; what proved difficult; how long each phase took. Closing note that the pattern log is the manual seed of the pattern catalogue.

**9 — Guardrails under a manual regime.** A strip of eight invariants that no tier exempts: classification determination, retention approval, security and access review, volume certification, promotion ladder, support acceptance, audit trail, new document type re-review. Then a comparison table — guardrail / how it is enforced in Phase 1 by a person / how it is enforced in Phase 2 by the platform — covering classification determination, retention approval, security and access routing, promotion ladder, new document type (caught at the fortnightly drift review, up to two weeks late), and audit trail. An accent band: the central discipline risk — a written rule applied by a person under schedule pressure is weaker than a gate that blocks; guardrails will be waived; the defence is visibility not prohibition, so every waiver is recorded with who authorised it and reviewed fortnightly. Then four watch-item cards: deep triage shortened; pool reclaimed by squads; tracker decays; manual Lean attempted too widely.

**10 — The twelve weeks.** A four-step chevron chain: weeks 1–2 Prepare / 3–6 Run in parallel / 7–10 Operate the tiers / 11–12 Evidence review, each with its activities. A note that the parallel run should not be shortened — it produces data at zero delivery risk and gives the leads four weeks of scoring practice before assignments bind real work. Then the evidence gate as a seven-row table (criterion / what good looks like / what it proves): distribution holds (50%+ to Lean or Lean+); lighter tiers hold (escalation below 25%); clarification cycles fall; partners accept assigned tiering (appeal below 20%, most unsuccessful); the baseline exists; the bottleneck is identified; no compliance regression. A navy band: the sixth criterion is the one that specifies the build.

## Design system

- 13.3 × 7.5 inches (`LAYOUT_WIDE`). Margins 0.45" left and right.
- Palette — navy `15304F` (primary, Dedicated), teal `0B6E6E` (Lean), amber `9A6410` (Lean+), plum `5B4A72` (Factory), rust `A44A2A` (accent / alert), green `2E6B4F` (positive), ink `1B2432`, muted `55606E`, slate `7C8798`, line `D4DAE2`, panel `F4F7FA`. Tints: `E6F2F1` / `FAF1DF` / `E8EDF4` / `F1EEF6` / `FBF0EB` / `E9EEF4`.
- Fonts — Cambria for titles and big numbers, Calibri for everything else. Nothing else.
- Every tier keeps the same colour on every slide, without exception. Tier cells in tables get a tinted background and coloured bold text.
- Consistent slide header: a small numbered navy square, a 24pt Cambria title, a 10.5pt muted subtitle, a right-aligned section tag (Context / What manual means / Preparation / The new triage / Tiering / The pool / The ceiling / Measurement / Guardrails / The plan), and a hairline rule beneath.
- Title slide: full navy background, a teal vertical bar at the far left edge, kicker in letter-spaced caps, large Cambria title, subtitle, a short teal rule, four metadata columns, and a footer rule. Keep the title short enough for one line.
- Rounded rectangles (radius ~0.05), soft shadows, hairline borders. No gradients, no accent stripes under titles, no decorative colour bars.

## Build instructions

Use `pptxgenjs` in Node. Set `pres.layout` before adding slides. Hex colours with **no** `#` and no alpha. Build a fresh options object for every `addShape`/`addText` — never share one. Shadow offsets must be ≥ 0. Use `charSpacing`, not `letterSpacing`. Put `isTextBox: true` on every `addText` and `margin: 0` wherever text must align to a shape edge. `rectRadius` only works on `ROUNDED_RECTANGLE`.

Write a shared helper library first — slide header, stat tile, card, segmented bar, legend, chevron chain, table (with tier-cell colouring), note band, callout band, section label — and compose all ten slides from it.

**Speaker notes are mandatory on every slide** via `slide.addNotes()`. Write them for someone running a working session with the team that will execute this: the mechanics, what to emphasise, where to invite challenge, and the specific instruction to give the room. Several hundred words per slide.

**QA before handing it over:** convert to PDF, render every slide to an image and inspect each one. Check first for text overflowing or cut off at a shape boundary — especially the last wrapped line inside bordered callout boxes, which is by far the most common failure. Then check for tables or cards colliding with the band below them, section labels wrapping onto the content beneath, content off the slide edge, and badges taller than their table rows. Fix everything found and re-render to confirm.

## Tone

Practitioner register — this is an operating guide, not a pitch. Be direct about what will be hard: that two leads will score the same intake differently at first, that guardrails will be waived under pressure, that the pool will be reclaimed unless protected, that the tracker will feel like overhead in week five. Address each with a named mitigation rather than optimism. End by asking the room to keep a running record of where the manual approach hurt, because that record is the Phase 2 case and nobody can reconstruct it from memory afterwards.
