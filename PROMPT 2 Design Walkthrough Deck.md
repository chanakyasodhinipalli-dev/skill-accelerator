# PROMPT 2 — Design Walkthrough Deck (architects and leads)

Copy everything below the line into a fresh session.

---

Build me a PowerPoint deck (.pptx) for a technical walkthrough with architects and enabling squad leads. Title slide plus **17 content slides**. Dense, infographic-led, data-carrying — this audience can absorb detail and will stress-test it. No bullet-point filler.

## Context you are working from

I lead work on an enterprise ECM platform team. ECM provides repository services to partner applications for storage, archival and records management, with metadata compliance and document classification applied on arrival. We also provide partner onboarding, API enablement and migration pipeline establishment. Three scrum teams, thirty-plus engineers, every intake delivered through a dedicated capacity model from inception to release.

**The structural problem:** Capacity required = concurrent intakes × a fixed engineering depth applied to every intake. Both terms sit outside our control, so headcount is the only lever. It is an allocation problem, not a throughput problem.

**Two independent causes.** (1) *The data deficit* — the requirement for a data point is discovered incrementally, after the intake has landed, costing days of elapsed time per cycle; squads estimate 30–40% of total elapsed time; tier-independent, so no re-tiering fixes it. (2) *Uniform allocation* — of 38 onboarding scenarios, 8 need no design, 14 need a judgement or two, 14 genuinely need a dedicated pod, 2 are migration campaigns; all 38 get the same team.

**The model:** tier the engagement, not the partner. Lean (self-service on a proven pattern, no allocated capacity, flat scaling), Lean+ (shared pool, two mandatory design checkpoints, named guide, sub-linear scaling), Dedicated (allocated pod, inception to release, linear scaling), plus a Migration Factory for remediation run as campaigns.

## Slide-by-slide specification

**1 — The problem, in one slide.** Navy band carrying the capacity equation. Two cards: Cause 1 (the data deficit, with its five properties) and Cause 2 (uniform allocation, including the uncomfortable line that without an objective rule depth gets allocated by influence). Below, a table of where elapsed time actually goes — waiting for partner responses, re-explaining the same requirement, rework from assumed answers, discovery sessions that cannot conclude, follow-up loops at triage, late-surfacing lead-time items — each with its mechanism and which models it affects. Note: validating the 30–40% costs almost nothing and is a Sprint 1 deliverable.

**2 — The Intake Data Catalogue.** 54 data points across 10 categories, shown as ten colour-barred rows in two columns, each with a letter, name, what it primarily unblocks, and a count badge: A Business context and outcome (5), B Application and zone identity (6), C Document inventory (8), D Trust and classification inputs (5), E Retention and regulatory (6), F Channel and integration (7), G Metadata and retrieval (5), H Security, access and users (4), I Environments, testing, cutover (4), J Support and ownership (4). Below: the seven things each catalogue entry holds (the question in partner language; why it is required; what it blocks, named as a gate; what a good answer looks like; diagnostic sub-questions for a partner who does not know; its completeness band; which patterns make it applicable). A navy band: *the why-register is the asset, not the chatbot* — roughly two weeks of two experienced engineers plus a governance analyst, and once written it serves the copilot, partner documentation, squad induction and the pattern catalogue.

**3 — How the Intake Copilot behaves.** Four numbered cards: (1) derives the question set before asking anything; (2) explains why at the point of asking; (3) helps a partner who does not know; (4) infers and shows its working. Then two tables. First, "the same requirement asked two ways" — left column the current terse question, right column the same question with its consequence attached (document traits → whether human review is needed, two to three weeks and a separate engagement; expected volume → three numbers, migration/steady/peak, the third decides load testing; retention → if regulatory rather than policy the content goes to a different repository with immutable storage, not easily reversed). Second, "when the partner does not know" — *I don't know* → offer realistic options with consequences; *not sure which applies* → diagnostic sub-questions; *something inconsistent* → surface the contradiction; *I need to ask someone* → register a dated gap with owner and blocking gate (flag this row as the one that eliminates the discovery cycle).

**4 — Completeness bands and the handoff contract.** Four band cards: Mandatory to accept (intake submission / not accepted into the queue), Mandatory to design (P1 gate / design baseline cannot close), Mandatory to provision (P2 gate / provisioning cannot complete), Mandatory to certify (P4 gate / promotion blocked) — each with typical content. An accent band carrying the distinction: a blank field versus "not yet known — owner: partner solution architect — required by the design checkpoint on 14 November — blocks: repository profile design". Then the handoff contract as eight paired items: validated answers, registered gaps, completeness score by band, derived scope, long-lead items, pattern match and delta, proposed tier with reasoning, provisional assessments. Closing note: today an engineer begins by working out what is missing; under the contract they begin by reading a list of it.

**5 — The three enablement models.** Three full-height cards with coloured headers. Each lists "what ECM provides" and "what the partner owns", and closes with a tinted strip stating the engagement shape (event-driven / scheduled / continuous). Beneath, a strip of eight accent-outlined invariants that no tier exempts: classification determination, retention approval, security and access review, volume certification, promotion ladder, support acceptance, audit trail, new document type re-review. Note: the argument to risk and audit is not that less rigour is applied, but that the same rigour is applied by the platform rather than by an engineer's attention — plus a spot-audit of completed Lean intakes.

**6 — Tiering: how an intake is routed.** The seven determinants as a 0–3 scoring table: D1 pattern precedent and maturity, D2 content trust posture, D3 regulatory and records impact, D4 classification engine change, D5 data model and metadata, D6 volume and performance, D7 breadth of impact. Thresholds: 0–5 Lean, 6–11 Lean+, 12–21 Dedicated, each with a one-line reading. Hard overrides forcing a tier regardless of score: pattern maturity below M3 → not Lean; below M1 → Dedicated; existing content or metadata remediated → Dedicated or Factory; new regulated retention or WORM → Dedicated; multiple LOBs or partners → Dedicated. Accent note on tier being assigned not chosen, with a one-step five-day appeal that must name the determinant, and repeated successful appeals on one determinant being a rubric defect rather than gaming.

**7 — The 38 scenarios, tiered.** Two segmented bars (at entry 8/14/14/2; steady state 16/13/7/2). Then ten representative rows with number, scenario, driving determinants, tier and escalation condition — include: #1 new Apigee subscription (Lean), #9 existing patterns new content same flows (Lean), #5 Apigee to Kafka ingestion (Lean+), #12 data model update (Lean+, Dedicated if historical), #22 untrusted non-regulated ingestion (Lean+), #14 legacy KOFAX to iDocs (Dedicated), #19 trusted and regulated (Dedicated, hard override), #25 multiple business units (Dedicated, hard override), #34 making non-compliant compliant (Dedicated, hard override), #30 RM remediation (Factory). Closing band: nine of fourteen Lean+ scenarios move to Lean once patterns reach M3; four Dedicated scenarios move to Lean+ once harvested; this table is therefore also the platform backlog, prioritised.

**8 — Drift, escalation and certification.** Escalation triggers table: new document type with no determination → Lean+; DKE coverage gap for untrusted types → Dedicated; volume breaches the load-testing threshold → Lean+; retention changes to regulated → Dedicated; historical content comes into scope → Dedicated; three consecutive failures on the same gate → Lean+; pattern variation outside declared tolerance → Lean+; a second line of business joins → Dedicated. Three cards: De-escalation (at phase gates, releases the pod, never retroactive), Appeal, and Certification (the gate on Lean — platform onboarding, one prior successful intake, nominated technical owner, acceptance of operating terms, good standing). Note that certification persists at partner level, so a new partner's first intake is never Lean.

**9 — Platform capability architecture.** Five stacked layers, numbered bottom-up: 1 Enablement record, 2 Pattern catalogue, 3 Provisioning engine, 4 Gate and policy engine, 5 Partner cockpit and clinic. Right column: a card mapping what replaces the engineer in Lean (knowledge of what to do next → pattern catalogue; authority to provision → provisioning engine with policy bounds; judgement that something is wrong → gate engine with explicit reasons; memory of what was agreed → enablement record; chasing → workflow and notification; document production → artefact generation) and a card of what the platform deliberately does *not* do. A navy band: *automate the decisions before automating the systems* — a rule returning the correct answer delivers most of an integration's value at a fraction of the cost, because the expensive part of integration is the connection, not the logic; generation beats integration for the same reason. Close with the implied build sequence as a chevron chain.

**10 — The pattern catalogue.** Left: the ten things a pattern contains (identity and version, applicability, step sequence, configuration template, required inputs, gate set, tolerated variation, escalation triggers, generated artefacts, precedent). Right: the maturity ladder M0 Observed → M1 Described → M2 Validated → M3 Self-service, each with which tiers it supports. Below: the five-step compounding loop (pod delivers a novel intake → pod produces a pattern candidate as a closure condition → platform engineering hardens it → pattern enters the catalogue at a maturity level → matching intakes become eligible for lighter tiers). A navy band: *this is the engine of the whole model* — Dedicated work is pattern acquisition, and a pod that delivers a partner but produces no reusable pattern has done half its job.

**11 — AI enablement and its guardrails.** The four-layer separation as four full-width rows, each with a coloured label block, a "DOES" column and a "MAY NEVER" column in the alert colour: **PROPOSE** (AI, grounded — may never decide, write to a system of record, or clear a gate), **DECIDE** (deterministic rules, codified policy — may never infer or approximate), **ACT** (platform execution within policy bounds — may never act on a proposal that has not become a decision), **AUTHORISE** (named humans — may never be delegated to the layers above). Then the ten capabilities as small cards: A Intake Copilot, B Pattern Matcher, C Tiering Assistant, D Scope Derivation, E Artefact Generator, F Gate Engine, G Onboarding Assistant, H Drift Detector, I Precedent Retrieval, J Clinic Triage — with a line noting the first four carry most of the benefit and none needs deep integration, and that F is explicitly *not* AI. Finally eight AI-specific guardrails. Closing note: the compliance concern is never that AI drafts a document, it is that it decides something consequential and nobody can reconstruct why.

**12 — The phase model.** A table of six phases (P0 Qualify / P1 Design / P2 Provision / P3 Build / P4 Validate / P5 Release) with their gate and what happens in each of Lean, Lean+ and Dedicated. A navy band: one row is identical in all three columns — promotion is a platform action in every tier, never a human one, and that uniformity is the entire compliance defence for the lighter tiers. Below, six small cards on where each phase is heaviest and why, highlighting that P1 is near-zero in Lean because the determinations were made when the pattern was built, and that P2 is the highest automation yield of any phase.

**13 — Guardrails.** Universal guardrails table (classification determination, retention approval, security and access, promotion ladder, new document type, audit trail) with what each enforces and how. Three tier-specific cards — Lean (certification prerequisite, M3 required, tolerated variation explicit and narrow, three-strike escalation, no silent exceptions, compliance spot-audit, clinic access guaranteed), Lean+ (both checkpoints mandatory, checkpoints require a completed artefact, the guide is a contact not a resource with time tracked and capped, escalation automatic not negotiated), Dedicated (pattern harvest is a closure condition, de-escalation at phase gates, tier justification recorded, queue position disclosed). Then guardrails on the model itself: escalation rate out of Lean above 20%; pattern debt; platform team raided for intake work on any occurrence.

**14 — Team structure.** Five functions as rows with a headcount badge and what each is measured by: Platform Engineering (9), Enablement Pods (10), Enablement Clinic (7), Migration Factory (4), Governance & Compliance Desk (3). A card on why three undifferentiated scrum teams cannot deliver it (everything competes with everything; nobody owns the platform, which is why patterns are not harvested; specialist governance knowledge spread thinly). A compact headcount table — 33 today, 33 target, redistribution not reduction. Transition sequencing as a four-wave chevron chain: Wave 0 months 0–3 (Desk and platform seed, rubric published, no Lean), Wave 1 months 3–9 (Clinic opens, Lean+ live, Lean stays closed), Wave 2 months 9–18 (Lean opens, pods formalised, Factory stands up), Wave 3 months 18+ (coverage broadens). Accent note on the sequencing rule that must not be broken: pattern maturity, not elapsed time, is the gate on tier availability.

**15 — The game plan, sprints 1 and 2.** Five team cards with allocation (product owner 1.0, platform engineers 2.0, pattern and content analyst 1.0, governance and records analyst 0.5, enablement engineer 1.0 from sprint 3). Two large sprint panels with coloured headers, a goal in italics, and eight deliverable rows each. Sprint 1 *Complete the intake*: intake data catalogue and why-register, intake copilot v1, completeness validation and banding, gap register, handoff contract, long-lead item flagging, clarification-cycle baseline, plus rubric published and retrospective scoring running in parallel. Sprint 2 *Make intake drive everything downstream*: scope derivation v1, tiering assistant, pattern matcher v1, precedent retrieval, top three patterns to M1, dual-run tiering begins, enablement record v0, phase baseline measurement. Close with exit criteria for both.

**16 — The game plan, sprints 3 and 4.** Same two-panel treatment. Sprint 3 *Guardrails, generation and the Clinic opens*: gate engine for P0 and P1, routing rules codified, artefact generator v1, partner onboarding assistant, clinic stands up, three patterns to M2, first Lean+ intakes, enabling squad onboarding. Sprint 4 *Prove Lean on one pattern*: self-service provisioning on one pattern, that pattern promoted to M3, partner certification live, cockpit v1, drift detector v1, leadership dashboard v1, first Lean intake completed, adoption push. Then eight "deliberately not attempted" cards with reasons (deep integration, all 38 patterns, a full partner portal, retiring existing tooling, migrating in-flight intakes, the Migration Factory, full team re-organisation, headcount commitments). Accent note on adoption: choose the first three partners deliberately — one high-frequency simple partner, one mid-complexity, and one known sceptic, because a model that only works with enthusiasts does not work; and never force a tier change on an in-flight intake.

**17 — Measures, risks and what good looks like.** A scorecard table of eight measures with why each is on the list, the position after four sprints, and the target at twelve months: clarification cycles per intake, intake completeness at handoff, tier distribution, intakes absorbed per period, intake-to-live elapsed time by tier, pattern maturity progression, pattern candidates per Dedicated closure, compliance findings on completed intakes. Then three failure-mode cards, each with the mechanism and its mitigation: the platform team is raided for delivery work; Lean opens before patterns are mature; patterns are not harvested. Close with a navy band carrying the three questions to ask at every checkpoint.

## Design system

- 13.3 × 7.5 inches (`LAYOUT_WIDE`). Margins 0.45" left and right.
- Palette — navy `15304F` (primary, Dedicated), teal `0B6E6E` (Lean), amber `9A6410` (Lean+), plum `5B4A72` (Factory), rust `A44A2A` (accent / alert), ink `1B2432`, muted `55606E`, line `D4DAE2`, panel `F4F7FA`. Tints: `E6F2F1` / `FAF1DF` / `E8EDF4` / `F1EEF6` / `FBF0EB` / `E9EEF4`.
- Fonts — Cambria for titles and big numbers, Calibri for everything else. Nothing else.
- Every tier keeps the same colour on every slide, without exception. Tier cells in tables get a tinted background and coloured bold text.
- Consistent slide header: a small numbered navy square, a 24pt Cambria title, a 10.5pt muted subtitle, a right-aligned section tag (Context / Intake completeness / Operating model / Architecture / Organisation / Game plan / Closing), and a hairline rule beneath.
- Title slide: full navy background, a teal vertical bar at the far left edge, kicker in letter-spaced caps, large Cambria title, subtitle, a short teal rule, four metadata columns, and a footer rule. Keep the title short enough to sit on one line.
- Rounded rectangles (radius ~0.05), soft shadows, hairline borders. No gradients, no accent stripes under titles, no decorative colour bars.

## Build instructions

Use `pptxgenjs` in Node. Set `pres.layout` before adding slides. Hex colours with **no** `#` and no alpha. Build a fresh options object for every `addShape`/`addText` — never share one. Shadow offsets must be ≥ 0. Use `charSpacing`, not `letterSpacing`. Put `isTextBox: true` on every `addText` and `margin: 0` wherever text must align to a shape edge. `rectRadius` only works on `ROUNDED_RECTANGLE`.

Write a shared helper library first — slide header, stat tile, card, segmented bar, legend, chevron chain, table (with tier-cell colouring), note band, callout band, section label — and compose all 17 slides from it. With this many dense slides, the helper library is what keeps it coherent.

**Speaker notes are mandatory on every slide**, via `slide.addNotes()`. Write them for someone walking a technical audience through the design: the argument, which element to point at, the objection to pre-empt, where to invite challenge. Several hundred words per slide.

**QA before you hand it over:** convert to PDF, render every slide to an image, and inspect each one. Check first for text overflowing or being cut off at a shape boundary — especially the last wrapped line inside bordered callout boxes, which is by far the most common failure. Then check for overlapping elements, tables colliding with the band below them, section labels that wrap onto the content beneath, content running off the slide edge, and badges taller than their table rows. Fix everything found and re-render to confirm.

## Tone

Technical register, direct, no hedging. This audience will stress-test the rubric and the architecture, so make the reasoning visible — in the scenario table, show the determinants that drove each assessment so the assessment is arguable on its merits rather than on authority. Where a number is an estimate rather than a measurement, say so on the slide. Be honest about what the re-organisation means for people, including that not everyone will want every role.
